About the clock

This clock tells the time two ways: the familiar 24 × 60 × 60, and the 48 × 48 × 48 clock described here. Either way, the short hand goes round once a day, midnight at the top and noon at the bottom, so the face can show where the daylight falls.

The 48 clock

The day is divided by 48, three times over:

dour1/48 of a day30 min
zan1/48 of a dour37.5 s
mec1/48 of a zan0.78125 s

The readout writes each unit as two dozenal (base-12) digits, where 2 (dek) is ten and 3 (el) is eleven, so each runs from 00 to 33 (forty-seven). Noon is 20:00:00.

What the face shows

The gold wedge is today's daylight, from sunrise to sunset where you are.

The green wedge, behind the gold, is the daylight of the longest day of the year, so the green showing past the gold is the daylight today is still short of.

The blue markers on the rim are sunrise and sunset on the shortest day of the year.

Black around the rim is the part of the day that is never in daylight on any day of the year.

Why 48?

48 = 2⁴ × 3, so it cuts up easily: it halves cleanly four times and splits into thirds as well. The same 48 serves at every level, where our clock switches from 24 to 60. And a dour is exactly half an hour, so it lines up with the clock we already have, and with nearly every time zone.

So why don't we use it?

The world already runs on 24 × 60 × 60, and the second is the unit science measures everything else by. Zans and mecs never come out even against it, 48 has no fifths or tenths, and dozenal digits are a second new habit on top of the first. France tried decimal time in 1793 and gave it up within two years.

So the 48 clock is offered here as something to look at the day through, not a replacement for the one on the wall.

The dozenal societies

Counting in twelves has had organized advocates for a long time. The Dozenal Society of America, founded in 1944, and the Dozenal Society of Great Britain, founded in 1959, have spent decades researching and teaching base twelve in arithmetic, in weights and measures and in timekeeping. The digits 2 and 3 come from that tradition: Isaac Pitman devised them, and the societies have long used them. Their work is at dozenal.org.

A clock in twelves

The societies' own way of dividing the day is by twelve, five times over, which makes the time a single dozenal number with one digit for each unit:

dwell1/12 of a day2 h
breather1/12 of a dwell10 min
trice1/12 of a breather50 s
lull1/12 of a trice4⅙ s
twinkling1/12 of a lullabout 0.35 s

The division goes back to the American society's founders in 1945; these unit names are newer. Paul Rapoport, a member of its board, has built it into a set of iPhone clocks, described in the second issue of the society's newsletter, Dozenal Jottings. His analog face also sends its short hand round once a day, and noon reads 600.

The two clocks fit together: a dwell is exactly four dours. The twelve clock needs only one digit for each unit. The 48 clock takes two, and in exchange gets three units instead of five, with one that lands on every half hour.
