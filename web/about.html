About the clock

This clock tells the time of day two ways: the familiar 24 × 60 × 60 (hours, minutes, seconds), and the 48 × 48 × 48 clock described here. Either way, the short hand goes round once a day, midnight at the top and noon at the bottom, so the face can show where the daylight falls.

The 48 clock

The day is divided by 48, and each part by 48 again, three times over:

dour1/48 of a day30 min
zan1/48 of a dour37.5 s
mec1/48 of a zan0.78125 s

A day is 48 dours, 2,304 zans or 110,592 mecs. The readout writes each unit with two dozenal (base-12) digits, where X is ten and E is eleven, so each one runs from 00 to 3E (forty-seven). Noon is 20:00:00.

Why 48?

48 = 2⁴ × 3, and that makes it easy to cut up. It halves cleanly four times (48, 24, 12, 6, 3), and it splits into thirds as well, so half, a third, a quarter, a sixth, an eighth, a twelfth, a sixteenth and a twenty-fourth of it are all whole numbers. Ten numbers divide 48 evenly: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24 and 48.

It also uses the same 48 at every level, where our clock switches from 24 to 60, so the hands and the readout all work the same way. And a dour is exactly half an hour, so it lines up with the clock we already have at every half hour, and with nearly every time zone.

So why don't we use it?

The case for: one rule all the way down instead of two; a day that halves, quarters and thirds into whole dours (a quarter of the day is 12 dours, a third is 16); a mec close enough to a second to count by; and a dour that converts to half-hours without arithmetic.

The case against: the world already runs on 24 × 60 × 60. Every clock, timetable, contract and law is written in hours and minutes, and the second is the unit science measures everything else by, so a new day would have to be converted at every border with the old one. A zan of 37.5 seconds and a mec of 0.78125 seconds never come out even against the second. 48 has no fifths or tenths, which 60 has, and reading the time in dozenal digits is a second new habit on top of the first. France tried decimal time in 1793 and gave it up within two years; habit is the strongest argument of all.

So the 48 clock is offered here as something to look at the day through, not a replacement for the one on the wall.

What the face shows

The gold wedge is today's daylight: it runs from sunrise to sunset where you are.

The green wedge, behind the gold, is the daylight of the longest day of the year, so the green showing past the gold is the daylight today is still short of the most there is.

The blue markers are lines across the rim, with a pointer outside the dial, at sunrise and sunset on the shortest day of the year: the least daylight there ever is.

Black around the rim is the part of the day that is never in daylight on any day of the year.
